The finding

One mechanism, everywhere

Every behaviour survey ranked the same thing first or second. A body puts a molecule into the shared medium. The medium spreads it and breaks it down. Anything standing there reads it, or is changed by it.

Emit

A body pays energy to release a molecule.

knob: cost per unit
Spread

The molecule diffuses through the medium, or stays stuck where it fell.

knob: diffusion
Decay

It breaks down, evaporates or gets eaten.

knob: lifetime
Read or act

A sensor reports it, or it changes the body of whoever is there.

knob: effect

Trails, warnings, digestion, weapons and family badges are all this loop with different settings. A trail and an alarm differ only in how far and how long the molecule travels. A food and a poison differ only in what it does to the reader. What a molecule means is whatever evolution wires to it.

Bacteria · how a cell eats

Fast and wasteful, or slow and efficient

A cell can burn the same sugar two ways. One gives more energy per molecule. The other gives more energy per unit of machinery. Which one wins depends on the world around it.

E. coli respiring glucose fully gets about 26 ATP per glucose. Overflowing to acetate gets about 12. But respiration needs roughly twice the protein machinery for the same ATP flux, so a fast-growing cell whose protein budget is full switches to the wasteful route Basan 2015.

The switch is sharp. Below a growth rate of about 0.76 per hour, no acetate leaves the cell. Above it, excretion rises in a straight line, whatever sugar sets the growth rate.

Theory and biochemistry agree on the social side: slow, high-yield burning acts like cooperation and survives in structured worlds, while fast, low-yield burning wins when everything is mixed Pfeiffer 2001. The waste is not wasted either. Once the sugar runs out, acetate is the next meal.

Digesting outside the body

Yeast cannot import sucrose, so it secretes invertase, which splits sucrose outside the cell. About 99% of the glucose made this way diffuses off before its maker can take it in Gore 2009.

That should make producers lose to cheaters who skip the enzyme. They don't, quite. Growth is strongly concave in glucose (benefit grows as glucose to the power 0.15), so the last 1% matters a lot to a starving producer. Producers and cheaters settle at a stable mix of about 30% producers. Wild yeast also switches the enzyme off above about 0.1% glucose: it cooperates when cheaters are around and defects when they are not.

The same physics governs bacterial exoenzymes and siderophores. In packed clumps the product stays local and producers win; in open, mixed water it becomes a commons Julou 2013 Drescher 2014.

Staying alive costs, and corpses pay back

Across environments, microbial rates of growth, maintenance and bare survival scale about 10⁶ : 10³ : 1, all following one activation energy Price & Sowers 2004. Deep subsurface cells turn over their biomass on timescales of centuries.

Starving E. coli die at 0.25 to 0.59 per day, and cells that grew slowly beforehand die slower. Dead cells feed the living at a recycling yield of 12 to 23%: about eight corpses make one new cell Biselli 2020.

Reserves split unevenly on purpose. A starving Sinorhizobium mother gives one daughter a lot of stored PHB (it can survive over a year without food) and one daughter little (it competes better now) Ratcliff & Denison 2010.

Microbes · weapons, cheaters and kin

Rock, paper, scissors in a dish

Three E. coli strains: one makes a toxin, one is killed by it, one is immune. Kept on a still plate, all three lived. Shaken in a flask, only one survived Kerr 2002.

The loop is non-transitive. The killer beats the sensitive strain. The sensitive strain outgrows the resistant one. The resistant one outgrows the slow killer. With local contact, patches chase each other around the plate forever. Mixing breaks it: the killer wipes out the sensitive strain everywhere at once, then the resistant strain beats the killer and is left alone.

Resistance usually means losing the outer-membrane receptor that the colicin enters by, and that receptor also imports nutrients. So immunity has a built-in price. Kerr's models show the community collapses if resistance or toxin production costs too much or too little.

Producers pay twice: the toxin, and part of themselves. The colicin leaves by lysing the cell that made it, a spiteful act whose payoff depends on how many neighbours are kin Gardner 2004.

Counting heads with a molecule

Vibrio fischeri makes a small autoinducer that crosses its envelope freely, so inside equals outside within about 20 s. Light genes switch on from about 10 nM and saturate near 200 nM Kaplan & Greenberg 1985. A cell cannot tell "many neighbours" from "small space" or "no flow", which is why Redfield argued it may be sensing whether its secretions will be lost Redfield 2002.

Making the signal costs. A Pseudomonas mutant that skips it outgrows its parent in a mix Sandoz 2007, and cheaters spread unless neighbours are kin Diggle 2007.

Family is a tag compared to a tag

In every system the surveys found, "recognising kin" is one protein binding another. Proteus swarms carry ids proteins whose binding is allele specific Cardarelli 2015. Myxococcus cells fuse outer membranes only when their TraA receptors bind each other, and a single amino acid flips who counts as self Cao & Wall 2017. Yeast FLO1 makes carriers stick to other carriers, whatever the rest of their genome says: a one-gene "greenbeard" Smukalla 2008.

Nothing in the molecule says "kin". A matching tag is accepted even on a stranger. Myxococcus strains from one centimetre of soil already fall into many recognition groups that do not track genetic distance Vos & Velicer 2009.

There is also a free route to helping relatives: stay put, and your neighbours are your offspring. But relatives that stay put also compete with each other, and the two effects can cancel exactly West 2002 Griffin 2004.

Fungi, slime molds and swimmers

Following a smell, remembering a path

There are two ways to climb a gradient. A small cell remembers what it smelled a moment ago. A big cell compares its front with its back.

E. coli weighs what it sensed in the last second positively and the three seconds before that negatively, then swims straighter if the difference is good Segall 1986. Methylation keeps resetting the baseline, so it reports change, not level, and makes the cell 35 times more sensitive Sourjik & Berg 2002. It is blind when it stops.

Dictyostelium compares across its own body and steers on a 1 to 5% front-to-back difference, about five receptors' worth Endres & Wingreen 2008. Neutrophils manage 1% Zigmond 1977. The limit is counting noise: the error falls as one over the square root of diffusion × size × concentration × time Berg & Purcell 1977.

Simolution units today can only do the first trick, with a DELAY node on LOCAL_RESOURCE. A neighbour-difference sensor would give them the second.

Writing memory onto the floor

Physarum leaves slime wherever it goes and avoids slime it touches. In a Y-maze with one slimed arm, 39 of 40 chose the clean arm Reid 2012. That turns a gradient-climber, which gets stuck in a dead end, into a searcher that does not go back. The memory costs no internal state; it lives in the world, where everyone else can read it too.

A non-diffusing field with its own decay is the whole mechanism. It is also how ants mark trails and how territory gets marked.

Starving amoebae become a wave

About 5 h after food runs out, Dictyostelium cells start to fire cAMP: at first every 15 to 30 min, then every 6 min as they gather. A cell fires when outside cAMP passes about 500 pM, and firing rate rises with density over flow Gregor 2010. Each pulse is repeated by neighbours, so a short-range signal becomes a long-range wave.

10⁴ to 10⁵ cells then form a slug. The front 20% become a stalk and die so the rest can disperse as spores. In half of the mixes of wild clones, one clone cheated its way out of stalk duty Strassmann 2000.

Who may fuse with whom

When two fungal hyphae fuse, their cytoplasm mixes. A short set of het loci checks the match. If any locus differs, the fused compartment and often its neighbours die within about 30 min Glass & Kaneko 2003. Ascomycetes carry 5 to 11 such loci with 2 to 4 versions each, so random strangers almost never pass. Even siblings mostly fail: 7.7% of Aspergillus sibling pairs were compatible Auxier 2024.

Fungal networks, Physarum tubes and plant-fungus trading all need bodies that are physically joined. That makes them the richest ideas here and also the most new physics.

Social insects · pheromones and stigmergy

The world as memory

An ant colony stores most of what it knows in the ground. Trails, marks and buildings are memories everyone can read, and they fade on a clock set by chemistry.

Give Argentine ants two bridges of equal length to a food source and the colony ends up on one of them, chosen by chance. Make one bridge shorter and the colony picks it, because round trips on it finish sooner and lay pheromone faster Goss 1989. The hero sketch at the top runs this loop.

Two features keep it from freezing. Noise: ants that make mistakes let the colony notice when the better food moves Dussutour 2009. And a second, faster channel: Pharaoh's ants put a "no entry" repellent at forks that led nowhere Robinson 2005.

Pure positive feedback with no exit has a failure mode. Army ants cut off from their column follow their own trail into a circle and march until they die Delsuc 2003. The same rule, at the scale of a healthy colony, runs columns of 200,000 foragers carrying over 3,000 prey an hour Couzin & Franks 2003.

Division of labour without a boss

Give each worker a threshold for each task. An undone task builds a stimulus (heat, hunger, a smell); workers with low thresholds start first, and the stimulus falls before high-threshold workers are needed. If doing a task lowers your own threshold for it, identical workers become specialists Theraulaz 1998. Bumblebees measured on fanning showed exactly that: consistent differences, and thresholds that fell with practice Weidenmüller 2004.

In Simolution a threshold is just a gene weight on a sensor. This one needs no new law, only a demand signal to respond to.

Signals cost little to make, so they stay honest only where interests line up, as among nestmates. Anything a colony broadcasts can be read by an enemy, which pushes alarm signals toward short range and fast decay Heinze & d'Ettorre 2009. For a simulator the lesson is plain: give emission a real energy cost, and honesty, cheating and eavesdropping can all evolve.

Environment · what happens regardless

What happens to a body anyway

Heat, poison, crowding and radiation act on a body whatever its brain decides. A stressor only creates new strategies if a lineage has some way to answer it.

Escape it

Sense it and move away. Needs a sensor for that field.

Endure it

Store energy or mass and outlast it. Simolution units can already do this.

Be built for it

A heat-tolerant or toxin-resistant body. In real organisms most tolerance is body, not behaviour, and every tolerance has a price.

Temperature sets the clock

Across 1,072 measured thermal responses, the rising side of the curve follows Arrhenius with an average activation energy of about 0.66 eV Dell 2011. That is a Q10 of about 2.3: ten degrees warmer, everything runs about twice as fast, upkeep included.

Above the optimum the curve does not taper, it falls off a cliff. So small overshoots kill and small undershoots only slow you down, which favours an optimum set safely below the hottest days.

The specialist-versus-generalist trade-off is real but loose. E. coli lines evolved at 20 °C lost 9.4% fitness at 40 °C on average, yet only 15 of 24 lines showed a significant loss and one gained Bennett & Lenski 2007. Heat-acclimated E. coli survived 50 °C better but competed worse at 41.5 °C Leroi 1994: readiness costs.

Poison, resistance and the commons

Growth falls along a sigmoid as a toxin rises, crossing zero at the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Resistance shifts the curve right but almost always costs growth when the toxin is absent Andersson & Hughes 2010.

Because the cost is small, resistant cells start winning long before the toxin is lethal: selection was seen at levels several hundred times below the MIC Gullberg 2011. A faint toxin field is a smooth slope to climb.

Breaking a toxin down is a public good. A strain that secreted β-lactamase kept 5 to 12% sensitive freeloaders alive in ampicillin; anchoring the enzyme to its own membrane shut them out Dugatkin 2005.

How the world fluctuates may matter more than any new law. When conditions change rarely, random switching beats sensing; when they change often, a fixed middle body wins; when a reliable cue comes first, sensing wins Kussell & Leibler 2005. Simolution's inflow language can already pulse and drift food. The same would work for any new field.

Resources

Different foods only add possibilities when their chemistry differs: one is another's waste, or burns faster but yields less. Two foods that behave the same are the same food twice.

Fluctuation

Changing any field's statistics over time is free: no new law, just inflow settings. The stressor survey ranked it second.

Open question

Can the genome shape the body? One cheap route: body traits as action nodes that physics reads and charges for. A fixed trait is a CONST connection; wiring a sensor in makes it plastic, like real acclimation. The gene format stays as it is. Not decided.

Suggested order

  1. Emit, plus local and gradient sensors per chemical. The biggest jump, reusing diffusion and decay.

  2. Death drops the body. Small, and predation later depends on it.

  3. Chemical effects on bodies, starting with damage and digestion.

  4. By-products and rate versus yield: the grounded version of "several resources".

  5. Body traits, then temperature, tags and stress mutagenesis.

Each step gets its own contract and is judged in the viable reference world.
