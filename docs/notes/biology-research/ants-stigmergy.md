# Social insects, pheromones, stigmergy and signal theory: primitives for a lattice simulator

## 1. Trail pheromone deposition and shortest-path selection (double bridge)

- Primitive: a walking ant lays a non-volatile or slowly volatile chemical on the substrate cell it occupies: C(x,t+1) = C(x,t) * (1 - lambda) + s * (ants present at x). Diffusion is negligible for substrate-bound trail pheromone (the trail is a line, not a plume). The ant reads it locally ahead of itself (see section 4) and its probability of taking a branch rises nonlinearly with the concentration on that branch. Deneubourg-type choice function: P(left) = (h + C_L)^a / ((h + C_L)^a + (h + C_R)^a), with h a detection threshold and a the nonlinearity; a > 1 (sigmoid) is required for one branch to win.
- Numbers: Goss et al. (1989) ran a nest-to-food bridge with two branches; with equal branches the colony ends up using one branch (random which), with a longer and a shorter branch the shorter is selected in most runs (abstract not retrievable; the exact percentages and branch ratio are unverified, a teaching slide gave about 50/50 for equal branches and about 100% shorter for unequal, which is a secondary summary). Choice function parameters from Deneubourg et al. (1990): the exponent and threshold values are unverified (a search for n = 2, k = 20 found no source; later papers say only that a > 1 is needed and the best-fit a changes between setups). Perna et al. (2012, PLoS Comput Biol, open access) re-measured Argentine ants and instead found a Weber-law turning response: turning angle proportional to the normalised left-right pheromone difference, detection threshold assumed 50 pheromone units, ant speed slightly under 2 cm/s, maximum turn about 35 degrees, response region about 1 cm ahead in two 90 degree front-left and front-right sectors, with pheromone proxied as ant passages (one unit per second per square millimetre, arbitrary scale).
- Trade-off: exploitation of the best known route against exploration of alternatives. Strong nonlinearity gives fast collective decisions and high lock-in; weak nonlinearity gives slow, flexible, multi-trail behaviour. Shortest-path selection works only if short routes receive more reinforcement per unit time (round trips complete sooner, so more pheromone accumulates early).
- Minimum capability: deposit onto the occupied cell while moving, read the field at one or two points ahead, a stochastic turn or branch rule that is nonlinear in the reading. No memory, no map, no carrying state is needed for the double-bridge result itself. Some rule for walking back toward the nest is needed for the loop to close (a returning state, or a second signal, or a nest gradient); the double bridge assumes ants know which end is home.

## 2. Positive feedback, lock-in, noise, U-turns and trail geometry

- Primitive: reinforcement loop. More traffic on a path means more deposit, which means more traffic. Lock-in follows from nonlinearity plus decay too slow to erase early advantages. Three additional primitives modulate it: (a) U-turn, an ant on a trail that turns back at a rate that depends on trail strength, path geometry and path length, (b) deposition modulated by food quality or crowding, (c) geometry, where the angle between branches at a fork tells an ant which way is home (a forager that finds itself walking the wrong way turns around when it meets a fork whose branches open toward it).
- Numbers: Beckers, Deneubourg and Goss (1992, Lasius niger) tested three mechanisms for shortest-path choice and found U-turns mattered far more than bidirectional laying; U-turn frequency depended on path geometry, length and trail strength; foragers showed no significant tendency to reuse their previous path (abstract via search snippet). Beckers et al. (1993): foragers on a 1 M sugar source laid 43 percent more trail marks than those on 0.05 or 0.1 M sources, and this modulation alone sufficed to select the richer source (abstract via search snippet, not confirmed against the paper). Czaczkes et al. (2014, L. niger): under trail crowding only 54 percent of ants deposited in the first two visits versus 93 percent without crowding (via search snippet). Jackson, Holcombe and Ratnieks (2004, Pharaoh's ant): correct reorientation at a fork was most frequent at a bifurcation angle of about 60 degrees, close to angles in natural networks (abstract read). Dussutour et al. (2009, Pheidole megacephala, not Pharaoh's ant): colonies could still choose the better of two food sources after conditions changed, and a stochastic model showed that noise (random errors) is what lets the colony track change (abstract read). Dussutour et al. (2004, Nature): under crowding a second trail forms before throughput drops, which keeps food return near optimal, with inhibitory interactions between ants central (abstract read; the species is not named in the Europe PMC record).
- Trade-off: lock-in makes the colony efficient but brittle when the environment changes. Noise (wrong turns, lost ants, imperfect following) is not a defect: it is the exploration channel. Crowding feedback turns a single trail into several automatically.
- Minimum capability: stochastic movement with a nonzero error rate, a turn-back (reversal) action, deposit rate that can depend on a local variable (own load, crowding in the neighbourhood). Geometry polarity needs only the ability to sense two forward cells separated by an angle and to reverse when the pheromone gradient geometry is wrong; no memory.
- Sources:

  - Beckers R, Deneubourg JL, Goss S (1993) Modulation of trail laying in the ant Lasius niger and its role in the collective selection of a food source. J Insect Behav 6:751-759. https://doi.org/10.1007/bf01201674 (abstract via search snippet; the 43 percent figure is as that snippet reported it).
  - Czaczkes TJ, Gruter C, Ratnieks FLW (2014) Rapid up- and down-regulation of pheromone signalling due to trail crowding in the ant Lasius niger. Behaviour 151:669-682. https://doi.org/10.1163/1568539x-00003157 (via search snippet).
  - Jackson DE, Holcombe M, Ratnieks FLW (2004) Trail geometry gives polarity to ant foraging networks. Nature 432:907-909. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03105 (abstract read).

## 3. Multiple pheromones with different lifetimes, and the negative "no entry" signal

- Primitive: more than one chemical channel written to the same cell, each with its own decay constant and its own sign of effect on the reader. Pharaoh's ant has at least three trail chemicals: a long-lasting attractive pheromone that forms a stable network, a short-lived attractive pheromone, and a short-lived repellent. The repellent is deposited at the fork leading to an unrewarding branch and biases the next ant's choice away from it. Pheidole megacephala uses a long-lived weak "exploration" trail and a short-lived strong "exploitation" trail.
- Numbers: Robinson et al. (2008, Pharaoh's ant): the repellent's behavioural effect lasted about 78 minutes versus about 33 minutes for the short-lived attractive effect; both decayed at about the same rate but the repellent's initial effect on branch choice was about twice as large (abstract via search snippet from the repository record). Jeanson et al. (2003): on a Y-fork, about 80 percent of ants followed the marked branch on plastic and about 70 percent on paper at the start; preference fell to 50 percent after about 25 minutes on plastic and about 8 minutes on paper; estimated half-lives about 9 and about 3 minutes (abstract via search snippet; a second search result gave the 25 and 8 minute figures as the time to 50 percent, so treat half-life and time-to-50-percent as not cleanly separated). Jackson et al. (2006): trails made by 1000 to 2000 ant passages could be re-found after 24 h and trails made by 4000 to 8000 passages after 48 h; only 27.5 percent of individual foragers were highly successful at detecting a 24 h old trail, and success correlated with a low antennal position (abstract via search snippet). Dussutour et al. (2009, P. megacephala): two pheromones, exploratory one longer lasting with weak recruitment, exploitation one shorter lasting with strong recruitment (abstract read). Perna et al. (2012) used a 30 minute half-life as a lower bound for Argentine ant trail pheromone and cite about 4 hours for gaster extracts (page text read; the 4 h source is theirs, not checked). A modelling paper quoted a 47 minute lifetime for Lasius niger from earlier experiments (secondhand, unverified).
- Trade-off: a long-lived channel is memory (a persistent map) but ages badly when the world changes; a short-lived channel is fast, adaptive and cheap to clear. Negative signals prune bad branches without needing the bad branch to be explored repeatedly. Persistence also determines how much a colony can tolerate gaps in traffic.
- Minimum capability: at least two independent chemical fields (or two channels in one field with separate decay), deposit actions per channel (a reader that distinguishes sign, attractive versus repellent), and a state or context in which each is laid (exploring versus exploiting, rewarded versus unrewarded at a fork). A returning-with-food flag or a "found nothing at this branch" memory of a few ticks is sufficient.
- Sources:
  - Robinson EJH et al. (2005) 'No entry' signal in ant foraging. Nature 438:442. https://doi.org/10.1038/438442a (abstract read; I did not verify the full author list).
  - Robinson EJH, Green KE, Jenner EA, Holcombe M, Ratnieks FLW (2008) Decay rates of attractive and repellent pheromones in an ant foraging trail network. Insectes Soc 55:246-251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00040-008-0994-5 (abstract via search snippet of the repository record).
  - Jeanson R, Ratnieks FLW, Deneubourg JL (2003) Pheromone trail decay rates on different substrates in the Pharaoh's ant. Physiol Entomol 28:192-198. https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-3032.2003.00332.x (abstract via search snippet from the Sussex repository).
  - Jackson DE, Martin SJ, Holcombe M, Ratnieks FLW (2006) Longevity and detection of persistent foraging trails in Pharaoh's ants. Anim Behav 71:351-359. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2005.04.018 (abstract via search snippet).
  - Dussutour A et al. (2009) The role of multiple pheromones in food recruitment by ants. J Exp Biol 212:2337-2348. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.029827 (abstract read).
  - Czaczkes TJ, Gruter C, Ratnieks FLW (2015) Trail pheromones: an integrative view of their role in social insect colony organization. ## 4. Minimal sensing for trail following: two sensors and spatial comparison (tropotaxis, osmotropotaxis)

- Primitive: the ant reads the field at two points (left and right antenna tips, spaced a few millimetres) and turns toward the higher reading. This is a spatial difference measurement; it needs no memory and no absolute concentration scale (the turn can be proportional to the normalised difference, (C_L - C_R) / (C_L + C_R)). With one sensor the ant must sample over time (klinotaxis, needs short memory of the previous reading and some weaving). Edge detection of a trail line is naturally a two-sensor task.
- Numbers: Draft et al. (2018, Camponotus pennsylvanicus): high-resolution video showed three tracking strategies (sinusoidal, probing, trail following); left and right antennae moved in opposite directions; during trail following the ants used both antennae to find trail edges and guide turns, described as a specialised form of tropotaxis; removing one antenna made tracking less accurate (abstract read). Perna et al. (2012): response region about 1 cm ahead in two 90 degree sectors, no sign of integrating pheromone over time (page text read). Hangartner (1967, Lasius fuliginosus) is the classic experiment on trail specificity, inactivation and orientation along the trail; I could not read it, so the antenna-spacing and crossed-trail results usually attributed to it are unverified here.
- Trade-off: two sensors give robust steering without memory but cost an extra sensor and make the controller more complex; one sensor plus DELAY is cheaper but needs weaving and loses on thin or curving trails. Sensor spacing sets the minimum resolvable trail width and the gradient scale an agent can use.
- Minimum capability: two read points at displaced positions relative to heading (so the unit needs a heading, or at least a pair of adjacent-cell reads), a subtraction or comparison in the controller, and a turn effector. A single-cell reader with one-tick memory is the weaker alternative.
- Sources:
  - Draft RW, McGill MR, Kapoor V, Murthy VN (2018) Carpenter ants use diverse antennae sampling strategies to track odor trails. J Exp Biol 221:jeb185124. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.185124 (abstract read).
  - Hangartner W (1967) Spezifitat und Inaktivierung des Spurpheromons von Lasius fuliginosus Latr. und Orientierung der Arbeiterinnen im Duftfeld. Z Vergl Physiol 57:103-136. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00303068 (bibliographic only).
  - Perna et al. (2012), see section 1.

## 5. Alarm pheromones: volatile, short-range, short-lived, and tied to a response

- Primitive: a burst release at a point source into the air above the substrate. Concentration spreads by diffusion and is lost by decay or advection: dC/dt = D * laplacian(C) - lambda * C with a large D and large lambda compared with trail pheromone. The signal is therefore a transient "active space" around the sender, bounded by a detection threshold, and it is gone within seconds to minutes. Receivers respond by moving toward the source (attack or recruitment) or away from it (panic), depending on species and concentration (formicine alarm-defence systems have both responses).
- Numbers: Moser, Brownlee and Silverstein (1968, Atta texana): workers detected 4-methyl-3-heptanone at 5.7e-13 g per cm3 and were alarmed at 5.7e-12 g per cm3, a tenfold gap between detection and alarm (figures via a search-returned summary of the paper; the abstract itself was not retrievable. The same snippet gave molecule counts of 2.7e7 and 2.7e8 per cm3, which do not match the mass figures for a compound of molecular weight 128, where my arithmetic gives about 2.7e9 and 2.7e10, so quote the gram figures only). Bossert and Wilson (1963) is the standard theory of "active space" (the region where concentration is above the response threshold, growing quickly after release and shrinking as decay wins); I could not retrieve the paper's radius and duration numbers, so any quoted radius or duration for ant alarm clouds is unverified here. Wilson and Regnier (1971) distinguish a panic and an aggressive alarm response in formicine ants (as cited by later work; the abstract was not retrieved).
- Trade-off: a fast-decaying, volatile signal is cheap to clear and does not leave stale information, but it works only at short range and for as long as the sender keeps emitting; a persistent signal reaches far in time but can mislead. Alarm signals are exactly what predators and parasitoids eavesdrop on (see section 13).
- Minimum capability: an emit action that writes a high-diffusion, high-decay field, a sensor that reads the local value, and a response that is conditional on level (low means approach, high means flee, or the reverse). No memory needed.
- Sources:
  - Moser JC, Brownlee RC, Silverstein R (1968) Alarm pheromones of the ant Atta texana. J Insect Physiol 14:529-535. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1910(68)90068-1 (bibliographic only; numbers via search snippet).
  - Bossert WH, Wilson EO (1963) The analysis of olfactory communication among animals. J Theor Biol 5:443-469. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(63)90089-4 (bibliographic only; PubMed has no abstract).
  - Wilson EO, Regnier FE (1971) The evolution of the alarm-defense system in the formicine ants. Am Nat 105:279-289. https://doi.org/10.1086/282724 (bibliographic only).
  - Blum MS (1969) Alarm pheromones. Annu Rev Entomol 14:57-80. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.en.14.010169.000421 (bibliographic only).
  - Verheggen FJ, Haubruge E, Mescher MC (2010) Alarm pheromones: chemical signaling in response to danger. Vitam Horm 83:215-239. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0083-6729(10)83009-2 (abstract read; also notes natural enemies use alarm pheromones to find prey).

## 6. Nestmate recognition: cuticular hydrocarbon profiles as a shared chemical template

- Primitive: each individual carries a surface chemical profile, a vector of many compound abundances (cuticular hydrocarbons, CHCs), partly genetic and partly environmental. A receiver compares what it detects on a contact partner to an internal template of its own colony's profile and acts (accept or attack) on the mismatch. The colony "gestalt" arises because members exchange and pool these compounds (grooming, trophallaxis, shared nest material), so the colony odour is a mixture that every member carries and every member's template is built from. Recognition is therefore comparison against a learned, updated reference, not a lookup of an identifier.
- Numbers: Martin and Drijfhout (2009) compared published profiles from 78 ant species across five subfamilies and found almost 1000 hydrocarbons in 187 homologous series in ten classes, most commonly n-alkanes, then monomethylalkanes, dimethylalkanes and alkenes (abstract via search snippet from a mirror page, check against the journal). Ozaki et al. (2005, Camponotus japonicus): a multi-pore antennal sensillum responds only to hydrocarbon blends of non-nestmates, so discrimination starts at the sensory periphery (abstract read). Lahav et al. (1999) give direct behavioural evidence that hydrocarbons act as the recognition cue (bibliographic only). Crozier and Dix (1979) analyse the genetic models of colony odour including the gestalt model (bibliographic only; I did not read it, the description above of pooled gestalt comes from the standard literature, not from this check). Threshold of mismatch for aggression: unverified here.
- Trade-off: strict recognition protects stores from exploiters and keeps cooperation among relatives, but costs errors (rejecting nestmates with shifted profiles) and is a target for chemical mimicry (see section 13). A broad template is permissive and exploitable; a narrow one is costly and fragile.
- Minimum capability: a heritable, mutable vector label per unit (a few channels of chemical identity), a way to read the label of the unit in contact (adjacent cell), a template that is updated from what the unit itself carries and from what it meets, and a conditional action (attack, ignore, share). Label mixing needs an exchange event between units.
- Sources:
  - Martin S, Drijfhout F (2009) A review of ant cuticular hydrocarbons. J Chem Ecol 35:1151-1161. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10886-009-9695-4 (abstract via search snippet).
  - Ozaki M et al. (2005) Ant nestmate and non-nestmate discrimination by a chemosensory sensillum. Science 309:311-314. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1105244 (abstract read).
  - Lahav S, Soroker V, Hefetz A, Vander Meer RK (1999) Direct behavioral evidence for hydrocarbons as ant recognition discriminators. Naturwissenschaften 86:246-249. https://doi.org/10.1007/s001140050609 (bibliographic only).
  - Crozier RH, Dix MW (1979) Analysis of two genetic models for the innate components of colony odor in social Hymenoptera. Behav Ecol Sociobiol 4:217-224. https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00297645 (bibliographic only).

## 7. Stigmergic building: the environment as memory and signal (termites, wasps, Lasius)

- Primitive: a build action that moves matter between cells (pick up and put down) and a marker mixed into the matter. Deposited material carries a "cement" pheromone that decays and diffuses, which raises the probability that others deposit nearby. Because deposition reshapes the local stimulus, the structure itself is the memory, and the next action depends on the current local state of the structure, not on any plan or history in the builder. In Bonabeau et al.'s termite model, pillar formation comes from positive feedback on the cement pheromone, and walls, galleries and royal chambers appear when four ingredients are added: air convection carrying the pheromone one way, a net directional termite flux, a self-maintained pheromone trail, and a pheromone template (the queen as a continuous source). The same individual rules produce different structures depending on those conditions.
- Numbers: Khuong et al. (2016, Lasius niger): two mechanisms were quantified: pheromone added to building material amplifies deposition at some locations (stigmergy), and body size acts as a template for the height at which ants start roofing pillars. The 3D stochastic model reproduced regularly spaced pillars, cap formation and merging, and remodelling, and showed pheromone lifetime to be a highly influential parameter for nest form (abstract read; numeric parameter values not in the abstract and unverified here). Bonabeau et al. (1998): the model is a modification of Deneubourg's earlier pillar model with the four additions above; lattice size and parameter values are not in the abstract and are unverified here (abstract read). Theraulaz and Bonabeau (1999) trace the term stigmergy to Grasse (1959) and give the standard example that the state of a structure triggers the next building action and that disturbing it makes the builder restart (summary via search snippet). Theraulaz and Bonabeau (1995) show that a lattice swarm model with local brick-placement rules can reproduce wasp nest architecture (bibliographic only; the abstract did not come back from search).
- Trade-off: building converts energy into a durable, shared, history-dependent information store; a structure outlasts any chemical trace and can even change the physics around it (shelter, flow, resource access), but it is costly, slow, and locks in the past (an inherited structure may become a trap). Without decay of the marker, structures freeze at their first pattern; with fast decay, nothing consolidates, so lifetime is a control parameter.
- Minimum capability: a carrying state (one load slot), pick-up and put-down actions that write to the world, a persistent per-cell state that others can sense, optionally a marker coupled to the deposited material. A queen or fixed source is not needed; a template can be the body or a physical gradient.
- Sources:
  - Grasse PP (1959) La reconstruction du nid et les coordinations interindividuelles chez Bellicositermes natalensis et Cubitermes sp. Insectes Soc 6:41-80. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02223791 (bibliographic only).
  - Bonabeau E, Theraulaz G, Deneubourg JL, Franks NR, Rafelsberger O, Joly JL, Blanco S (1998) A model for the emergence of pillars, walls and royal chambers in termite nests. Phil Trans R Soc B 353:1561-1576. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1998.0310 (abstract read on PMC1692383).
  - Theraulaz G, Bonabeau E (1999) A brief history of stigmergy. Artif Life 5:97-116. https://doi.org/10.1162/106454699568700 (via search snippet).
  - Theraulaz G, Bonabeau E (1995) Coordination in distributed building. Science 269:686-688. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.269.5224.686 (bibliographic only).
  - Khuong A et al. (2016) Stigmergic construction and topochemical information shape ant nest architecture. PNAS 113:1303-1308. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1509829113 (abstract read).
  - Perna A, Theraulaz G (2017) When social behaviour is moulded in clay: on growth and form of social insect nests. J Exp Biol 220:83-91. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.143347 (bibliographic only).

## 8. Army ant mills: a death spiral from a trail-following rule

- Primitive: the same positive-feedback trail rule as section 1, with a loop. If followers can only follow the trail and cannot sense the nest or direction, a group that becomes separated from the main raid column and meets its own trail forms a closed circuit; each lap lays more pheromone, which strengthens the circuit (no negative signal, no goal sensor, no exit rule). Ants run in the circle until they die of exhaustion. In the Deneubourg et al. raid model, army-ant raid patterns are self-organised from chemical trail laying and following by essentially blind ants, and Franks et al. (1991) tested its assumptions in the field.
- Numbers: Delsuc (2003, PLoS Biology primer): a mill starts when a group is cut off from the main column and its pheromone communication is disrupted (Schneirla 1944); the ants run in a tight circle until they die of exhaustion (Schneirla 1971); Couzin and Franks (2003) found the direction-choice and circle formation to be self-organised from simple local movement rules (text read; the primer gives no mill diameter, period or duration). Couzin and Franks (2003, Eciton burchelli): colonies reach up to 200,000 foragers moving more than 3000 prey items per hour along raiding columns longer than 100 m, and a model shows real army ants operate in the region of parameter space where lanes form and traffic flow is maximised (abstract read). Franks et al. (1991): running velocity is a sigmoid function of trail pheromone strength (abstract via search snippet). Mill circumference and rotation period figures commonly quoted from Beebe (1921) are unverified here (a search returned about 370 m circumference and 2.5 hours per lap only from Wikipedia, which I do not cite).
- Trade-off: the specialisation that makes trail following cheap (no map, no goal sensing, extreme speed) is exactly what makes mills possible: a pure reinforcement rule has an absorbing failure state. In an evolutionary simulation this is a real failure mode: any lineage that follows its own pheromone without a termination or an independent anchor can trap itself, so selection will press toward a decay rate, a negative signal, a speed limit or a second cue.
- Minimum capability: trail deposition, trail following, a position update every tick, no memory and no home sensor. The mill needs no extra mechanism. Lane formation needs a turning rate and a local perception range (Couzin and Franks).
- Sources:
  - Delsuc F (2003) Army ants trapped by their evolutionary history. PLoS Biol 1:e37. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.0000037 (text read on PMC261877).
  - Schneirla TC (1944) A unique case of circular milling in ants, considered in relation to trail following and the general problem of orientation. Am Mus Novit 1253. https://digitallibrary.amnh.org/items/5f80439c-e431-4c18-9e5e-81fed85fce39/full (existence and title confirmed by search result; content not read).
  - Deneubourg JL, Goss S, Franks N, Pasteels JM (1989) The blind leading the blind: modeling chemically mediated army ant raid patterns. J Insect Behav 2:719-725. https://doi.org/10.1007/bf01065789 (bibliographic only).
  - Franks NR, Gomez N, Goss S, Deneubourg JL (1991) The blind leading the blind in army ant raid patterns: testing a model of self-organization. J Insect Behav 4:583-607. https://doi.org/10.1007/bf01048072 (abstract via search snippet).

## 9. Division of labour: response thresholds and their reinforcement

- Primitive: each unit has an internal threshold per task; a task generates a stimulus S (a field or a local quantity that grows when the task is undone and falls when it is done). The probability of engaging is a rising sigmoid of S relative to the threshold: P = S^n / (S^n + theta^n). Fixed-threshold model: specialisation exists only because thresholds differ between individuals (castes). Reinforcement model: doing a task lowers that individual's threshold for it and not doing it raises it, so initially identical units become specialists, and removing specialists leaves a colony that does not return to its prior state if they come back late.
- Numbers: Bonabeau, Theraulaz and Deneubourg (1996): fixed thresholds can account for observations on Pheidole ants when castes carry different thresholds (abstract via search snippet; the response function parameters are unverified). Theraulaz, Bonabeau and Deneubourg (1998): variable thresholds with two-way reinforcement produce specialists from identical workers and make predictions about how specialisation develops over time and after removal of specialists (abstract read). Weidenmuller (2004, Bombus terrestris): thresholds for fanning measured on 118 workers (temperature) and 88 workers (CO2) in four colonies; workers differed consistently, thresholds decreased over successive trials (support for self-reinforcement), temperature and CO2 thresholds were not consistently correlated inside an individual, and response probability and duration behaved as independent parameters (abstract via search snippet). Gordon (1996) and Beshers and Fewell (2001) are the reviews of alternative mechanisms (encounter-rate based switching and others), bibliographic only.
- Trade-off: thresholds buy robustness and flexible allocation without any central control, at the cost of idle specialists and slow reallocation after disturbance. Variability between individuals is the resource that makes the colony adaptive; identical units cannot specialise without a symmetry-breaking reinforcement.
- Minimum capability: access to a shared or local task-demand signal, an inherited or learned per-unit threshold (a gene weight can play this role directly), a per-unit counter or state that changes the threshold with experience (needs memory), and at least two alternative tasks.
- Sources:
  - Bonabeau E, Theraulaz G, Deneubourg JL (1996) Quantitative study of the fixed threshold model for the regulation of division of labour in insect societies. Proc R Soc B 263:1565-1569. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1996.0229 (abstract via search snippet).
  - Theraulaz G, Bonabeau E, Deneubourg JL (1998) Response threshold reinforcements and division of labour in insect societies. Proc R Soc B 265:327-332. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1998.0299 (abstract read on PMC1688885).
  - Weidenmuller A (2004) The control of nest climate in bumblebee (Bombus terrestris) colonies: interindividual variability and self reinforcement in fanning response. Behav Ecol 15:120-128. https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arg101 (abstract via search snippet).
  - Beshers SN, Fewell JH (2001) Models of division of labor in social insects. Annu Rev Entomol 46:413-440. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ento.46.1.413 (bibliographic only).
  - Gordon DM (1996) The organization of work in social insect colonies. Nature 380:121-124. https://doi.org/10.1038/380121a0 (bibliographic only).

## 10. Trophallaxis: food and chemical sharing as a network

- Primitive: a contact event between two adjacent units in which a fraction of one unit's stored energy (and any carried chemical or label) moves to the other. It is a conservative transfer (no energy created) over a contact network. Because the carried fluid also holds hydrocarbons, proteins, hormones and small RNAs, the same event transmits identity cues (section 6) and developmental signals along with energy.
- Numbers: Buffin et al. (2009, Formica fusca, radio-labelled sucrose): after one hour 70 percent of the final harvest had entered the nest; total foraged amount was nearly four times below the colony's expected storage capacity; all ants were fed within 30 minutes but about 8 percent of the radioactive area held more than 25 percent of the stored food, concentrated in the centre of the aggregate, which stayed in place for three hours; the colony fed 200 ants within 30 minutes; intake dropped in proportion to food already collected (abstract read). LeBoeuf et al. (2016, Camponotus floridanus): trophallactic fluid contains digestive and non-digestive proteins, hydrocarbons, microRNAs and juvenile hormone; supplementing workers with the hormone made the larvae they raised about twice as likely to complete metamorphosis and grow into larger workers (abstract read). Greenwald et al. (2015): method to track food reserves of every ant through mouth-to-mouth transfers with barcodes and fluorescent food (abstract read; no results numbers in abstract). Dussutour and Simpson (2009): ants act as a collective mouth and gut; they improved the macronutrient balance of collected food by extracting carbohydrate and ejecting protein, and processing excess protein shortened lifespan, much less so with larvae present (abstract read). Sendova-Franks et al. (2010) studied famine relief by trophallactic networks in Temnothorax (bibliographic only, abstract not read).
- Trade-off: sharing smooths supply for the colony (starving units get fed, intake self-regulates to need) but opens the door to freeloaders and spreads pathogens along the same network. A few hub units can hold much of the store, so the network is highly unequal.
- Minimum capability: adjacency contact, an energy store above a self-need level, a transfer action (give), a rule for when to give (a threshold on own store or on partner state), and ideally a carrying channel for a label or a signal.
- Sources:
  - Buffin A et al. (2009) Feeding and stocking up: radio-labelled food reveals exchange patterns in ants. PLoS ONE 4:e5919. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0005919 (abstract read).
  - LeBoeuf AC et al. (2016) Oral transfer of chemical cues, growth proteins and hormones in social insects. eLife 5:e20375. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.20375 (abstract read).
  - Greenwald E, Segre E, Feinerman O (2015) Ant trophallactic networks: simultaneous measurement of interaction patterns and food dissemination. Sci Rep 5:12496. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep12496 (abstract read).
  - Dussutour A, Simpson SJ (2009) Communal nutrition in ants. Curr Biol 19:740-744. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2009.03.015 (abstract read).
  - Sendova-Franks AB et al. (2010) Emergency networking: famine relief in ant colonies. Anim Behav 79:473-485. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2009.11.035 (bibliographic only).

## 11. Recruitment by waggle dance and other non-chemical recruitment (bees)

- Primitive: a symbolic, directional message that encodes a vector (direction and distance) as a stereotyped motor pattern, read by followers in contact with the dancer. It is a channel distinct from a diffusing chemical: it is read only by units adjacent to the sender, and the information (a location) is arbitrary relative to the local environment. Why it evolved at all is still debated.
- Numbers: none verified. Dornhaus and Chittka (2004) ask whether the dance is advantageous at all, and I read nothing beyond the title and Crossref metadata. Any dance-code constants (angle to direction, duration to distance) are unverified here.
- Trade-off: a vector message lets a colony recruit to a target it could never reach by following a gradient or trail (no trail laid in air), but it requires a decoder in every receiver and a shared reference (gravity, sun), and the sender needs memory of a remote location. A high-cost, high-information signal.
- Minimum capability: memory of a remote position, a sender able to produce a repeatable pattern, receivers able to read and decode the pattern and a shared reference frame.
- Sources:
  - Dornhaus A, Chittka L (2004) Why do honey bees dance? Behav Ecol Sociobiol 55:395-401. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-003-0726-9 (bibliographic only).
  - Leonhardt SD et al. (2016) Ecology and evolution of communication in social insects. Cell 164:1277-1287. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2016.01.035 (abstract read; the review covers reproduction, membership, resources and threats as message classes).

## 12. Costly signalling and honesty

- Primitive: a signal is a cost-bearing action or trait whose cost depends on the sender's state; honesty is stable when the marginal cost (or marginal benefit) of a given signal level differs between high and low quality senders, so that low quality senders do better by signalling less. Zahavi's handicap: the signal is wasteful and costly and so tests the signaller. Grafen's strategic model shows a signalling equilibrium exists when the cost gradient is steeper for the low-quality sender. In social insects the honesty argument is different: workers in a colony share genes, so honest communication is expected for alerts and recruitment, while conflict (queen versus worker over reproduction) can favour dishonest signals, and fertility pheromones are debated as queen control versus honest queen signal.
- Numbers: Grafen (1990) vindicates Zahavi's claims inside an ESS model: if the handicap conditions hold a stable signalling equilibrium exists, and every equilibrium satisfies the conditions; honest signals evolve if low-quality males pay larger marginal costs than high-quality ones (abstract via search snippet; later criticism by Szamado argues that signalling cost at equilibrium is neither necessary nor sufficient for honesty, honesty can also come from differential benefits; I did not read that paper). Maynard Smith and Harper (1995): terminology paper separating signals from cues and indices, with self-reporting versus other-reporting and minimal versus cost-added signals (secondary summaries only). Maynard Smith (1991) Philip Sidney game: a model where a signal is honest only if cost is set against the relatedness of the interactants (bibliographic only). Heinze and d'Ettorre (2009): in social Hymenoptera cheating is not expected when nestmates are alerted or recruited, but conflict makes dishonest communication potentially advantageous, and many studies show differing pheromone blends between reproductive and non-reproductive individuals (abstract read). Keller and Nonacs (1993): queen control or queen signal (bibliographic only; I could not read the abstract). No numerical cost measurement for an ant pheromone signal was verified.
- Trade-off: cheap signals (a pheromone puff costs little) are honest only when interests align (kin) or when the signal is hard to fake (a physical byproduct of the state). Where interests diverge, cheap signals are exploited and receivers evolve discounting or ignoring. Costs can be metabolic (synthesis), risk (being found by eavesdroppers) or opportunity (time not foraging).
- Minimum capability: an emit action with a real energy cost, a receiver with a response that can be selected for or against, heritable variation in sender and receiver strategies, and a way for receivers to pay a cost for being fooled. Without a cost on emission the kernel cannot distinguish honest from cheap talk; no semantics are needed, only the cost accounting.
- Sources:
  - Zahavi A (1975) Mate selection, a selection for a handicap. J Theor Biol 53:205-214. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(75)90111-3 (abstract via search snippet).
  - Grafen A (1990) Biological signals as handicaps. J Theor Biol 144:517-546. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5193(05)80088-8 (abstract via search snippet).
  - Maynard Smith J (1991) Honest signalling: the Philip Sidney game. Anim Behav 42:1034-1035. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0003-3472(05)80161-7 (bibliographic only).
  - Maynard Smith J, Harper DGC (1995) Animal signals: models and terminology. J Theor Biol 177:305-311. https://doi.org/10.1006/jtbi.1995.0248 (bibliographic only).
  - Heinze J, d'Ettorre P (2009) Honest and dishonest communication in social Hymenoptera. J Exp Biol 212:1775-1779. https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.015008 (abstract read).
  - Keller L, Nonacs P (1993) The role of queen pheromones in social insects: queen control or queen signal? Anim Behav 45:787-794. https://doi.org/10.1006/anbe.1993.1092 (bibliographic only).

## 13. Eavesdropping, mimicry and exploitation of pheromone channels

- Primitive: any emitted chemical is a public field: whoever reads it can respond. Two exploitation types: (a) eavesdropping, a third party reads the signal and uses it to find senders (predators, parasitoids), and (b) mimicry or camouflage, an outsider emits or carries a copy of the colony profile and passes the recognition test. In both, the signal's active space and its persistence set the exposure.
- Numbers: Morrison and King (2004, Pseudacteon tricuspis on Solenopsis invicta): fire ant workers release alarm pheromone in aggressive encounters with non-nestmates, which likely attracts phorid flies from a distance; flies were attracted mainly to aggressive encounters, almost never to workers foraging at baits (via search snippet of the abstract). A follow-up study found the alarm pheromone component 2-ethyl-3,6-dimethylpyrazine attracted flies at all doses tested, and that alarm pheromone and venom alkaloids together attract more than either alone (an additive effect), with no response to recruitment-gland (Dufour's gland) extracts in a recent study (all via search snippets; the specific papers were not individually retrieved, so I cite only the Morrison and King DOI). Akino et al. (1999): the Maculinea rebeli butterfly is a social parasite of Myrmica colonies and uses chemical mimicry plus host specificity (title only, abstract not retrievable). Lenoir et al. (2001): parasites enter ant colonies by being chemically inconspicuous at takeover and then copying the host colony odour, and some produce chemicals that pacify or repel hosts (abstract read). Verheggen et al. (2010): natural enemies use alarm pheromones to find prey (abstract read).
- Trade-off: any signal that works also lights up the sender. This pushes signals toward short active space and fast decay, toward encrypted or compound blends, and toward private recognition cues. It also creates an arms race: receivers that discount cheap or exploited signals create selection for new channels.
- Minimum capability: a field that other species (or other lineages) can read, a sensor that reads it, a payoff from locating the sender (the eavesdropper gains energy), and for mimicry a label or profile that can be copied imperfectly plus a template comparison on the host side.
- Sources:
  - Morrison LW, King JR (2004) Host location behavior in a parasitoid of imported fire ants. J Insect Behav 17:367-383. https://doi.org/10.1023/b:joir.0000031537.41582.d1 (abstract via search snippet).
  - Akino T, Knapp JJ, Thomas JA, Elmes GW (1999) Chemical mimicry and host specificity in the butterfly Maculinea rebeli, a social parasite of Myrmica ant colonies. Proc R Soc B 266:1419-1426. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1999.0796 (title and metadata only).
  - Lenoir A, d'Ettorre P, Errard C, Hefetz A (2001) Chemical ecology and social parasitism in ants. Annu Rev Entomol 46:573-599. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ento.46.1.573 (abstract read).
  - Verheggen et al. (2010), see section 5.

## Top five for a simulator

1. Persistent trail field with decay plus a nonlinear, noisy two-sensor follower (sections 1, 2, 4): one substrate field, one deposit action, one gradient read; yields shortest-path selection, lock-in, trail-based foraging, mills, and a tunable exploration/exploitation knob from decay rate alone.
2. A second field with different decay and opposite sign (section 3): the fast repellent or short-lived channel next to a long-lived one adds pruning, tracking of change and memory-versus-reaction strategies for almost no new physics beyond a second chemical.
3. Carrying and put-down of matter with per-cell persistent state (section 7): stigmergic building turns the world into memory and shelter, opens niche construction and spacing patterns (pillars, walls), and its decay of marker lifetime controls form.
4. Costly volatile emission with eavesdropping by other lineages (sections 5, 12, 13): a high-diffusion, high-decay field with an energy cost per emit gives alarm, recruitment, honest-versus-cheap signalling and parasitism from one primitive, and exposes the cost accounting that makes honesty selectable.
5. Contact transfer of energy and a heritable surface label compared to a self template (sections 6, 10): trophallaxis plus label recognition enables kin sharing, exclusion of cheats, mimicry arms races and network structure; response thresholds (section 9) fall out for free once units have a tunable gene weight on a demand signal.
