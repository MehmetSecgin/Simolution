# Environmental stressors acting on microorganisms: primitives for a law-only simulator

Scope: environmental physics that happens to a body regardless of its decisions, with the heritable trait that lets lineages tolerate, resist or escape it, and the trade-off that stops the trait from maxing out.

## 1. Temperature as a rate multiplier (Boltzmann-Arrhenius, Q10)

- **Primitive**: Ambient temperature T (a field over the lattice, possibly changing in time) multiplies every biochemical rate of a body by f(T) = exp(-E/k (1/T - 1/Tref)), E the activation energy in eV, k = 8.617e-5 eV/K. One scalar field, one multiplier, applied to all rate laws (maintenance, harvest, growth, move, replication clock) or to a chosen subset. Q10 = f(T+10)/f(T) = exp(E/k (1/T - 1/(T+10))).
- **Numbers**: The metabolic-rate model of Gillooly et al. fits microbes, ectotherms, endotherms and plants over 0 to 40 C, with mass- and temperature-compensated rates all within a factor of about 20 [A] (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1061967). Dell, Pawar and Savage compiled 1,072 thermal responses (112 traits, 309 species): 87% of rising limbs fit Boltzmann-Arrhenius, mean E = 0.66 +/- 0.05 eV, with right skew around a median of 0.55 eV [A] (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1015178108). The theory is laid out in Brown et al. 2004 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1890/03-9000). [C] At 25 C, E = 0.66 eV gives Q10 about 2.3 (E/k = 7,660 K, 1/298 - 1/308 = 1.09e-4, exp(0.83)); E = 0.55 eV gives Q10 about 2.0. This matches the usual 2 to 3. Price and Sowers found growth, maintenance and survival rates of microbial communities all fit one activation energy of about 110 kJ/mol (1.14 eV) with rates scaled about 10^6 : 10^3 : 1, and no evidence of a minimum temperature for metabolism [A] (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0400522101). [C] 110 kJ/mol gives Q10 about 4.2 at 25 C, steeper than within-species growth rises, because it is a cross-community fit including cold ice.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: The Arrhenius form is fixed physics. What is heritable is the reference rate at a chosen temperature and, through enzyme stability, where the curve turns over (section 2). Dell et al. report that E itself varies systematically (skew) rather than being universal, so E can be treated as weakly evolvable, but the cheap and well-supported evolvable axes are the rate at Tref and the high-temperature cutoff [A].
- **Trade-off**: Hotter is better: genotypes adapted to higher optimum temperature have higher peak growth rate (tested in phages, Knies et al. [A], https://doi.org/10.1086/597224). In a closed-energy world, a rate multiplier also multiplies maintenance and cost per tick, so warm bodies burn through reserves faster at the same mass. A warm patch is rich in throughput and poor in persistence.
- **Why it matters**: One multiplier field makes space into a metabolic clock. Cold patches slow everyone (low turnover refugia), warm patches speed everyone (fast-lived, high-cost strategies), and a temporal cycle makes timing a strategy. No behaviour is coded.
- **Sources**: Gillooly 2001 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1061967 ; Brown 2004 https://doi.org/10.1890/03-9000 ; Dell 2011 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1015178108 ; Price and Sowers 2004 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0400522101 ; Knies 2009 https://doi.org/10.1086/597224

## 2. Thermal performance curve and cardinal temperatures

- **Primitive**: Realised rate = rising Arrhenius limb times a steep high-temperature collapse. Three cardinal temperatures per lineage: Tmin (no growth below), Topt (peak), Tmax (no growth above, often within a few degrees above Topt). Above Tmax there is also direct heat death (first-order protein and membrane damage per tick, rate rising steeply with T). The curve is left-skewed: slow rise, abrupt fall. Reference curve shapes: Ratkowsky square-root model sqrt(r) = b (T - Tmin)(1 - exp(c (T - Tmax))) [R] (https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.154.3.1222-1226.1983, title verified "Model for bacterial culture growth rate throughout the entire biokinetic temperature range", [M]); the Sharpe-Schoolfield enzyme-inactivation form [R] (https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(81)90246-0, [M]); the cardinal-temperature model with inflection of Rosso et al. [R], whose paper reports an unexpected correlation among the cardinal temperatures of a species (https://doi.org/10.1006/jtbi.1993.1099, [M]). The combined temperature and pH model uses three cardinal values for each axis plus one optimum rate, seven parameters in total, and assumes the two effects multiply [A] (https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.61.2.610-616.1995).
- **Numbers**: Cardinal values vary by organism over tens of degrees (psychrophile to hyperthermophile). I did not verify a single universal width, so none is stated. Deutsch et al. show that, for insects, fitness curves are asymmetric and organisms living close to Topt are the most sensitive to further warming [A] (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0709472105).
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: the asymmetric shape and the existence of a steep high-T collapse. Heritable: the position of Topt and Tmax along the temperature axis, peak rate at Topt, and breadth (section 3).
- **Trade-off**: Shifting Topt upward raises maximum rate but, in the generalised hotter-is-better picture, also raises Tmin (Knies et al. above [A]). Pushing Tmax further out costs stability (chaperones, saturated membranes, section 4).
- **Why it matters**: A lethal asymmetry (small excess is catastrophic, small deficit is only slow) selects for a safety margin below the true optimum when temperature fluctuates. This produces "optimum below the peak" strategies with no coding.
- **Sources**: Ratkowsky 1983 https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.154.3.1222-1226.1983 ; Schoolfield 1981 https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(81)90246-0 ; Rosso 1993 https://doi.org/10.1006/jtbi.1993.1099 ; Rosso 1995 https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.61.2.610-616.1995 ; Huey and Kingsolver 1989 https://doi.org/10.1016/0169-5347(89)90211-5 [M] ; Deutsch 2008 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0709472105

## 3. Thermal breadth: generalist vs specialist, and thermal adaptation in experiments

- **Primitive**: Each lineage carries (Topt, breadth, peak). The area under the curve (a notional "thermal budget") may be constrained, so a wider curve lowers the peak. The environment samples T; fitness is the mean log-growth over the temperatures it visits.
- **Numbers**: E. coli lines evolved for about 200 generations at 42 C improved temperature-specific fitness through de novo mutations [A] (Bennett, Dao and Lenski 1990, https://doi.org/10.1038/346079a0). In 24 lines evolved 2,000 generations at 20 C, the mean fitness decline at 40 C was 9.4%, but only 15 of 24 lines showed a significant trade-off, one line gained at 40 C, and the size of the gain at 20 C did not predict the size of the loss at 40 C [A] (Bennett and Lenski 2007, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0702117104). So a trade-off is general but not universal and not quantitatively tight. Cooper and Lenski found that most loss of unused catabolic functions in 12 E. coli lines happened early, when beneficial mutations were fixing, and mutator lines did not lose more [A] (https://doi.org/10.1038/35037572): evidence that loss of unused abilities can come from antagonistic pleiotropy and not only from drift. The hotter-is-better and broader finding in phages says the breadth-versus-peak trade-off is not guaranteed [A] (Knies 2009 above). Gilchrist's models and Kassen's review treat when generalists beat specialists in changing environments [M] (https://doi.org/10.1086/285797 ; https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1420-9101.2002.00377.x).
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: the environment's temperature statistics. Heritable: Topt, breadth, peak. A hard "area" constraint is a modelling choice; the experiments say the constraint is soft and lineage-specific.
- **Trade-off**: Specialists win in constant or predictable temperature (higher peak), generalists win when temperature wanders. A soft constraint (most lines pay, some do not) keeps diversity high and lets rare "free lunch" mutants exist.
- **Why it matters**: Gives a niche axis (temperature) along which lineages can coexist spatially (warm and cold patches) or temporally (seasons), the most direct route to stable coexistence.
- **Sources**: Bennett 1990 https://doi.org/10.1038/346079a0 ; Bennett and Lenski 2007 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0702117104 ; Cooper and Lenski 2000 https://doi.org/10.1038/35037572 ; Knies 2009 https://doi.org/10.1086/597224 ; Gilchrist 1995 https://doi.org/10.1086/285797 [M] ; Kassen 2002 https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1420-9101.2002.00377.x [M] ; Angilletta 2002 https://doi.org/10.1016/S0306-4565(01)00094-8 [M]

## 4. Heat shock and cold shock: damage, protective response, cost

- **Primitive**: When T exceeds a lineage's comfortable range, proteins misfold at a rate that rises steeply with T. Damage is a per-tick quantity (misfolded fraction or an "injury" state) that raises maintenance (repair, proteolysis) and, if it passes a threshold, kills. A protective response (chaperones, "heat shock proteins") can be induced, which lowers the damage rate but costs energy and slows growth while expressed. Cold shock: a sudden drop in T freezes membrane fluidity and slows translation; cells induce cold-shock proteins and adjust membrane lipids.
- **Numbers**: Heat shock proteins are an ancient, highly conserved chaperone network that prevents aggregation and refolds proteins [A] (Richter, Haslbeck and Buchner 2010, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2010.10.006). For E. coli, acclimation at 41.5 C increased survival at a lethal 50 C but reduced competitive fitness at 41.5 C relative to 32 C-acclimated cells, probably a burden of stress-protein expression [A] (Leroi, Bennett and Lenski 1994, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.91.5.1917). Feder and Hofmann review the ecological and evolutionary physiology of heat-shock proteins, including costs [M] (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.physiol.61.1.243). Cold shock: Jones and Inouye [M] (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2958.1994.tb00359.x). Membrane lipid adjustment to temperature (homeoviscous adaptation) is reviewed and questioned by Hazel [M] (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ph.57.030195.000315). I have no verified percentage for the energetic cost of the response.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: damage kinetics (unfolding rate vs T), the existence of a lethal threshold. Heritable: induction threshold, induction strength, basal level of protection, membrane composition. A "sensor" gene that reads local T could gate the response, but the response cost itself is law.
- **Trade-off**: Constitutive protection is paid every tick whether or not heat arrives (Leroi et al. [A]). Induced protection is cheap in calm times but lags the stress (acclimation delay), so a sudden spike kills the slow inducer. This is the classic cost-of-readiness versus cost-of-delay trade-off.
- **Why it matters**: Gives a reason for inducible, memory-bearing responses (DELAY nodes) to be selected, and makes the speed of the internal response a fitness trait.
- **Sources**: Richter 2010 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2010.10.006 ; Leroi 1994 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.91.5.1917 ; Feder and Hofmann 1999 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.physiol.61.1.243 ; Jones and Inouye 1994 https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2958.1994.tb00359.x ; Hazel 1995 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ph.57.030195.000315

## 5. Thermotaxis

- **Primitive**: A local temperature gradient is a sensor input (own-cell T versus remembered T through DELAY, like chemotaxis). It is not a law on the body; it is an input channel and a real physical signal. The law is only that T varies in space and time.
- **Numbers**: In E. coli, cells grown below a critical culture concentration swim toward warmth in a gradient; above it, toward cold; a secreted signal (glycine) mediates the switch through methylation of the Tsr receptor [A] (Salman and Libchaber 2007, https://doi.org/10.1038/ncb1632). In the absence of attractants E. coli has a steady thermophilic response whose magnitude decreases at higher temperatures, and adaptation to chemoattractants can invert the response at intermediate temperatures [A] (Paulick et al. 2017, https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.26607). Maeda et al. 1976 describe temperature effects on motility and chemotaxis [M] (https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.127.3.1039-1046.1976).
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: the gradient exists and is physically sensed through the same receptor machinery as chemicals. Heritable: sign and gain of the response, its dependence on density, its link to movement.
- **Trade-off**: Moving costs energy and mass-scaled move cost (already law in Simolution). Gradient following needs a memory (DELAY), and wrong sign drives the cell into lethal heat. The density-dependent sign flip shows the same gene circuit can encode "go to warmth when alone, to cold when crowded".
- **Why it matters**: A second gradient to climb on top of the resource field creates conflicts between gradients (food in the warm patch, safety in the cold patch), which is where nontrivial movement policies come from.
- **Sources**: Salman and Libchaber 2007 https://doi.org/10.1038/ncb1632 ; Paulick 2017 https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.26607 ; Maeda 1976 https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.127.3.1039-1046.1976

## 6. pH: tolerance range and the cost of holding cytoplasmic pH

- **Primitive**: Ambient pH is a field. A cell keeps cytoplasmic pH near neutral by pumping protons, and the pumping work grows with the gap between outside and inside pH (the proton gradient is part of the energy store of the membrane). Growth rate versus pH is a cardinal curve (pHmin, pHopt, pHmax). Outside the range, cytoplasm acidifies or alkalinises and growth stops.
- **Numbers**: The cardinal pH model uses three values, the pH below which no growth occurs, the pH above which no growth occurs and the optimum, combined multiplicatively with temperature [A] (Rosso et al. 1995, https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.61.2.610-616.1995). E. coli can survive a move from pH 7 to the pH 2 of the stomach through regulated acid-resistance systems [A] (Foster 2004, https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro1021). Reviews on cytoplasmic pH homeostasis in bacteria and archaea, alkaline homeostasis, molecular pH sensing, and acidophile homeostasis: Slonczewski 2009 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1016/s0065-2911(09)05501-5), Padan 2005 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbamem.2005.09.010), Krulwich 2011 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro2549), Baker-Austin and Dopson 2007 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tim.2007.02.005). I did not verify a specific ATP cost per unit of pH gap.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: the pH field and the proton-gradient bookkeeping. Heritable: pump capacity, set point of cytoplasmic pH, acid-resistance system level, membrane permeability.
- **Trade-off**: Bigger pump capacity widens tolerance but is a per-tick maintenance cost and may reduce mass-specific yield. A leakier membrane is cheap but lets the gradient collapse. Acidophiles have tolerance at the price of narrow range elsewhere [M].
- **Why it matters**: pH is a natural second niche axis, and (important for a closed energy world) metabolic by-products acidify their own surroundings, so growth changes pH and pH then limits growth (see section 16).
- **Sources**: Rosso 1995 https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.61.2.610-616.1995 ; Foster 2004 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro1021 ; Slonczewski 2009 https://doi.org/10.1016/s0065-2911(09)05501-5 ; Padan 2005 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbamem.2005.09.010 ; Krulwich 2011 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro2549 ; Baker-Austin 2007 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tim.2007.02.005

## 7. Osmotic stress and water activity

- **Primitive**: Ambient solute concentration sets water activity aw. Water flows through the membrane toward the side with lower aw; a cell in high external osmolarity loses water and turgor, and in low osmolarity swells and can burst. The cell compensates by accumulating compatible solutes (osmolytes) to rebalance, which costs energy to import or make and costs mass or reserve. Growth rate falls with aw and stops at a lower limit.
- **Numbers**: The lowest water activity that supports cell division is about 0.605 (Xeromyces bisporus; some halophilic archaea and bacteria are reported close to it) [A] (Stevenson et al. 2015, https://doi.org/10.1111/1462-2920.12598). Sugar-tolerant fungi grow near aw 0.61; haloarchaea grow in saturated NaCl at aw 0.75; most organisms use organic compatible solutes, a restricted group uses KCl [A] (Grant 2004, https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2004.1502). Reviews of E. coli osmoregulation: Wood 2011 [A] (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-micro-090110-102815); Record et al. 1998 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1016/s0968-0004(98)01196-7). Unverified: specific cost in ATP per osmolyte.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: water flux follows the aw gradient; the lower aw limit of known life. Heritable: osmolyte uptake and synthesis capacity, wall stiffness, channel density.
- **Trade-off**: Osmolyte accumulation means locking reserve into a non-metabolisable pool (analogous to mass without growth utility) and import transporter cost. High tolerance means slower growth in benign conditions.
- **Why it matters**: Cheap to add: one more field with diffusion and decay (salt, sugar), with evaporation-like concentration in patches. Because inflow can be programmed, salt gradients create niche boundaries.
- **Sources**: Stevenson 2015 https://doi.org/10.1111/1462-2920.12598 ; Grant 2004 https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2004.1502 ; Wood 2011 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-micro-090110-102815 ; Record 1998 https://doi.org/10.1016/s0968-0004(98)01196-7

## 8. Toxin and antibiotic dose response: Hill curve, MIC, hormesis

- **Primitive**: A diffusing, decaying toxin field C(x,y,t). Per-tick net growth or kill rate is a Hill (sigmoid E-max) function of C: psi(C) = psi_max - (psi_max - psi_min) (C/zMIC)^kappa / ((C/zMIC)^kappa - psi_min/psi_max). In words: net growth rate falls from the drug-free rate to a negative floor as concentration rises, MIC is where net growth crosses zero, kappa sets steepness. The simpler law-ready form: damage per tick = Vmax C^n / (K^n + C^n), added to the cell's energy bill or mass loss.
- **Numbers**: Regoes et al. fit a four-parameter Hill-function pharmacodynamic function to five antibiotic classes (ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, streptomycin, rifampin) in E. coli, showing that drugs differ not only in MIC but also in steepness and in the minimum net growth rate reached at high concentration [A] (https://doi.org/10.1128/AAC.48.10.3670-3676.2004; the displayed formula above is the form as I recall it, flagged [R]). Selection for resistant bacteria occurs at concentrations up to several hundred-fold below the MIC of susceptible cells, so the "selective window" extends far below MIC [A] (Gullberg et al. 2011, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.ppat.1002158). Low-dose stimulation with high-dose inhibition (hormesis) is reported as broadly general [A] (Calabrese and Baldwin 2003, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.pharmtox.43.100901.140223). Andersson and Hughes review sublethal-level effects [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro3270). Density modifies efficacy (inoculum effect): modest or substantial reductions in kill rate and increases in MIC with higher cell density for all six antibiotics tested in S. aureus [A] (Udekwu et al. 2009, https://doi.org/10.1093/jac/dkn554).
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: dose-response shape, the field dynamics, density effect. Heritable: the position of the curve (effective MIC) via target change, uptake, efflux, enzymatic breakdown.
- **Trade-off**: See section 9. Any rightward shift is paid for.
- **Why it matters**: A toxin field with sub-MIC leakage is a smooth selection gradient, so the population climbs resistance gradually and can do so by many routes. Hormesis is optional.
- **Sources**: Regoes 2004 https://doi.org/10.1128/AAC.48.10.3670-3676.2004 ; Gullberg 2011 https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.ppat.1002158 ; Calabrese 2003 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.pharmtox.43.100901.140223 ; Udekwu 2009 https://doi.org/10.1093/jac/dkn554 ; Andersson and Hughes 2014 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro3270

## 9. Resistance: cost, efflux, compensation, collateral sensitivity

- **Primitive**: A resistance trait multiplies the effective local toxin concentration by 1/R (R > 1: efflux or target change) and reduces the lineage's growth rate or yield by a cost c(R) paid every tick. Efflux pumps export toxin at an energy price per unit exported, so a pump consumes a fraction of the cell's budget in proportion to toxin load. Compensatory mutations lower c without lowering R. Resistance to drug A may raise or lower sensitivity to drug B (collateral sensitivity).
- **Numbers**: Most resistance mechanisms carry a fitness cost typically seen as reduced growth rate, and the size of the cost drives how fast resistance spreads and how fast it is lost [A] (Andersson and Hughes 2010, https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro2319). A meta-analysis of single resistance mutations found them generally costly, though several drug classes and species showed no average cost [A] (Melnyk, Wong and Kassen 2015, https://doi.org/10.1111/eva.12196). Compensatory mutations restore fitness usually without losing resistance, and the mutations selected depend on whether evolution happened in mice or in laboratory medium [A] (Björkman et al. 2000, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.287.5457.1479). Efflux pumps are the main multidrug-resistance route in Gram-negatives (Nikaido 2009 [M] https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.biochem.78.082907.145923; Nikaido and Pagès 2012 [M] https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1574-6976.2011.00290.x). Fitness cost of efflux-overexpressing Pseudomonas mutants: Sánchez et al. 2002 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1093/jac/dkf185). Collateral sensitivity networks have been used to design drug cycling [M] (Imamovic and Sommer 2013, https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.3006609; Pál et al. 2015, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tim.2015.02.009); Baym et al. 2016 review ways to select against resistant mutants [A] (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aad3292). Specific percentage costs per mutation vary widely; I did not verify a representative figure, so none is stated.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: cost is a physical consequence of the mechanism (pump energy, altered target function). Heritable: resistance level R, pump expression, compensation. A law could tie c to R through a convex function so no lineage can reach unlimited R.
- **Trade-off**: Cost in the absence of toxin keeps resistance from fixing everywhere; the cost can fall by compensation, so reversion is slow (Andersson and Hughes [A]). Collateral sensitivity means a refuge from one toxin may be a death trap under another.
- **Why it matters**: Two toxins with opposite collateral effects make cycles and coexistence of resistant and sensitive lineages, a very strong source of diversity per unit of physics.
- **Sources**: Andersson and Hughes 2010 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro2319 ; Melnyk 2015 https://doi.org/10.1111/eva.12196 ; Björkman 2000 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.287.5457.1479 ; Nikaido 2009 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.biochem.78.082907.145923 ; Nikaido and Pagès 2012 https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1574-6976.2011.00290.x ; Sánchez 2002 https://doi.org/10.1093/jac/dkf185 ; Imamovic 2013 https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.3006609 ; Pál 2015 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tim.2015.02.009 ; Baym 2016 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aad3292

## 10. Tolerance and persistence versus resistance

- **Primitive**: Three different things: resistance raises MIC; tolerance lengthens survival time under a lethal dose without changing MIC (slower killing); persistence is a small subpopulation that survives because it is dormant or slow-growing, and that regrows with the same sensitivity. Law-level implementation: kill rate is proportional to growth rate (fast-growing cells die first). A cell in low-activity state takes less damage per tick.
- **Numbers**: Persisters are a fraction of a genetically homogeneous population with reduced growth rate; cells regrown from persisters remain sensitive; switching between normal and persister state is stochastic and preexisting, shown for single E. coli cells [A] (Balaban et al. 2004, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1099390). The definitions of resistance, tolerance and persistence are set out in Brauner et al. 2016 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro.2016.34). In in vitro evolution experiments tolerance preceded resistance in all cases, and a population-genetics model showed tolerance boosts the spread of resistance mutations [A] (Levin-Reisman et al. 2017, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaj2191). I did not verify persister fractions.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: damage is rate-dependent. Heritable: probability of entering and leaving the low-activity state, depth of dormancy.
- **Trade-off**: A dormant cell neither grows nor reproduces, so persister fraction is paid as lost growth in good times. Optimal switching rate matches how often the lethal event arrives (section 18).
- **Why it matters**: A dormancy state emerges from nothing except "damage scales with activity", yielding bet hedging with no extra law.
- **Sources**: Balaban 2004 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1099390 ; Brauner 2016 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro.2016.34 ; Levin-Reisman 2017 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaj2191

## 11. Detoxification as a public good (cross-protection)

- **Primitive**: A lineage that degrades or sequesters toxin lowers the local toxin field. The toxin field is diffusing and shared, so neighbours benefit regardless of genotype. The producer pays the enzyme cost; the benefit goes to the whole neighbourhood.
- **Numbers**: Yurtsev et al.: with a beta-lactamase plasmid, the population settles to an equilibrium resistant fraction proportional to antibiotic concentration divided by cell density, independent of the starting fraction, and adding a beta-lactamase inhibitor made resistance spread [A] (https://doi.org/10.1038/msb.2013.39). Dugatkin et al.: a shared TEM-1 beta-lactamase strain kept sensitive cells at 5 to 12% in ampicillin, and lowering its cost raised its equilibrium frequency; a membrane-anchored (self-limited) version excluded sensitive cells [A] (https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2004.2916). Lee et al.: a few highly resistant E. coli mutants raised population survival by producing indole, which switched on efflux and oxidative-stress defences in others, at a fitness cost to the producers [A] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09354). Medaney et al.: in ordinary batch culture there was little evidence resistant cells protect sensitive neighbours, but at high inoculum densities dormant survivors grew near resistant colonies [A] (https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2015.154). Sorg et al. report collective resistance by intracellular deactivation [M] (https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.2000631). Note the mixed evidence: cooperation depends on density, enzyme secretion and spatial structure.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: field diffusion and decay, the shared nature of the field. Heritable: enzyme expression (producer or not), secreted or anchored (leaky or private).
- **Trade-off**: Classic cheater problem. Sensitive cells free-ride, so producers do not fix. Equilibrium fraction depends on toxin level and density (Yurtsev [A]). Spatial structure (lattice) can favour producers because the benefit stays local.
- **Why it matters**: On a lattice with local diffusion this yields cooperators and cheaters with no coded sociality, plus spatial patterns (protective halos).
- **Sources**: Yurtsev 2013 https://doi.org/10.1038/msb.2013.39 ; Dugatkin 2005 https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2004.2916 ; Lee 2010 https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09354 ; Medaney 2016 https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2015.154 ; Sorg 2016 https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.2000631

## 12. Oxygen and redox: fuel and poison

- **Primitive**: An oxygen field O(x,y,t). Aerobic respiration yields far more energy per unit substrate than fermentation but converts a fixed small fraction of O2 flux into reactive oxygen species (superoxide, hydrogen peroxide) inside the cell, which damage enzymes and DNA. Damage rate rises with intracellular oxidant level, so it rises with O and with respiratory flux. Scavenging enzymes and repair lower net damage at an energy cost. Anaerobes lack or lose these defences and are harmed by O.
- **Numbers**: Molecular oxygen adventitiously abstracts electrons from redox enzymes, continuously forming superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, which destroy metalloenzyme activities and DNA integrity, forcing scavenging and repair; elevated oxidants quickly poison bacteria [A] (Imlay 2013, https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro3032). Imlay's earlier review, "Pathways of oxidative damage" [M] (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.micro.57.030502.090938), and Messner and Imlay 1999 on primary sites of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide formation in the aerobic respiratory chain [M] (https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.274.15.10119). Fenchel and Finlay on oxygen and the spatial structure of microbial communities [M] (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-185x.2008.00054.x). Energetic comparison of anaerobic chemotrophs: Thauer, Jungermann and Decker 1977 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1128/br.41.1.100-180.1977). Caveat: the claim that bactericidal antibiotics kill via ROS (Kohanski et al. 2007 [A], https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2007.06.049) was directly contradicted (Liu and Imlay 2013 [A], https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1232751), so do not use antibiotic-induced ROS as a law. Unverified: a single universal fraction of O2 converted to ROS.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: ROS formation tied to respiratory flux, yield difference between respiration and fermentation. Heritable: respiratory versus fermentative pathway usage, scavenger and repair investment, the ability to switch pathway by O sensing.
- **Trade-off**: Aerobic strategy gives more energy per resource but higher oxidative damage and defence cost; anaerobic strategy is safe but low yield. Defence investment pays only if oxygen is present often enough.
- **Why it matters**: Oxygen gradients (high at surface, depleted inside dense clumps by the respiration of neighbours) create stratified niches without any new rule, because consumption already makes the gradient.
- **Sources**: Imlay 2013 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro3032 ; Imlay 2003 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.micro.57.030502.090938 ; Messner and Imlay 1999 https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.274.15.10119 ; Fenchel and Finlay 2008 https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-185x.2008.00054.x ; Thauer 1977 https://doi.org/10.1128/br.41.1.100-180.1977 ; Kohanski 2007 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2007.06.049 ; Liu and Imlay 2013 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1232751

## 13. Radiation and UV damage

- **Primitive**: A flux field of UV (or ionising radiation) hits cells at a rate set by position and time. Each tick, damage events to DNA occur as a Poisson process with mean proportional to flux times exposed area (mass^(2/3) or similar). Damage is repaired at an energy cost per lesion; unrepaired lesions cause death or become mutations at replication. A shielding term (depth in a clump, pigment) lowers dose.
- **Numbers**: UV induces cyclobutane-pyrimidine dimers and 6-4 photoproducts, two of the most abundant mutagenic and cytotoxic lesions; photoreactivation (photolyase), nucleotide excision repair and base excision repair are the main repair routes, with mutagenic bypass as a last resort [A] (Sinha and Häder 2002, https://doi.org/10.1039/b201230h). In E. coli the SOS response after UV upregulates several dozen LexA-controlled genes (26 documented lexA-regulated regions and 17 additional responsive sites found by microarray) [A] (Courcelle et al. 2001, https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/158.1.41). Repair in bacteria: Goosen and Moolenaar 2008 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dnarep.2007.09.002). Unverified: lesions per joule, survival curves.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: lesion formation proportional to dose. Heritable: repair capacity (energy spent), photolyase-like light-driven repair (a free repair that needs the same light), pigment shielding, genome size (larger target).
- **Trade-off**: Repair machinery is a per-tick cost and does not pay in a dark world. Error-prone bypass saves the cell but raises the mutation rate (section 14). Shielding costs mass or reserve.
- **Why it matters**: A moving or pulsing UV field is a cheap "predator-like" hazard that selects for shelter-seeking, timing and repair economics.
- **Sources**: Sinha and Häder 2002 https://doi.org/10.1039/b201230h ; Courcelle 2001 https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/158.1.41 ; Goosen and Moolenaar 2008 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dnarep.2007.09.002

## 14. Stress-induced mutagenesis

- **Primitive**: Mutation rate at birth is mu = mu0 * (1 + g * S), where S is a stress state (DNA damage, starvation, high toxin, heat) that the body carries as an internal quantity, and g is the gain. In Simolution terms this multiplies the existing point-mutation and indel rates at replication. It is a "law" that stress state modulates replication fidelity, since stress up-regulates error-prone polymerases and down-regulates repair.
- **Numbers**: Stress responses (RpoS, RpoH, ppGpp, LexA, polyphosphate) include functions that raise genetic variability: error-prone polymerases up, error-correction down, mobile elements moved, producing a transient mutator state [A] (Foster 2007, https://doi.org/10.1080/10409230701648494). A large fraction of E. coli natural isolates showed strong mutagenesis in aging colonies (MAC), which depends on starvation, oxygen, RpoS and cyclic AMP regulons and varies widely across isolates [A] (Bjedov et al. 2003, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1082240). Galhardo, Hastings and Rosenberg review regulation of evolvability [M] (https://doi.org/10.1080/10409230701648502); Cairns et al. 1988 is the origin of the debate [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/335142a0). In E. coli adapting to high ethanol stress, hypermutation evolved independently in different cultures, population mutation rate rose under stress and fell again once cells were adapted, and cell mortality was the main driver [A] (Swings et al. 2017, https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.22939). A model shows when stress-induced hypermutation is favoured in asexual populations [M] (Ram and Hadany 2012, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.2012.01576.x). Unverified: fold-increase values (the literature quotes large fold changes, but I did not verify a number).
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: the physical link stress, damage, polymerase switching. Heritable: the gain g and which stress channels couple to it. Founder genomes can lose or keep a mutagenesis response.
- **Trade-off**: High g produces beneficial variants for stressed lineages but, because most mutations are deleterious, a mutator state is itself a load (see section 15). Coupling to stress limits the cost to hard times: the "fitness-associated mutation rate" idea.
- **Why it matters**: Makes the rate of exploration depend on how badly a lineage is doing, so evolution speeds up exactly where it is most needed, giving punctuated rather than steady change in stressed patches.
- **Sources**: Foster 2007 https://doi.org/10.1080/10409230701648494 ; Bjedov 2003 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1082240 ; Galhardo 2007 https://doi.org/10.1080/10409230701648502 ; Cairns 1988 https://doi.org/10.1038/335142a0 ; Swings 2017 https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.22939 ; Ram and Hadany 2012 https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.2012.01576.x

## 15. Evolution of the mutation rate itself

- **Primitive**: The mutation rate at birth is a function of a heritable repair or fidelity trait (the existing mutation parameters already exist in the kernel as law constants; making them depend on a gene is the heritable version). A mutator allele raises mu by a multiplier; because it is linked to the beneficial mutations it generates, it can hitchhike.
- **Numbers**: Across DNA-based microbes, the mutation rate per genome per replication is nearly constant at about 0.0033, while rate per base pair varies about 16,000-fold with genome size [A] (Drake 1991, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.88.16.7160; further data in Drake et al. 1998 [M] https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/148.4.1667). Spontaneous mutators arose in E. coli populations adapting to a new environment over long-term experiments, in agreement with simulations [A] (Sniegowski, Gerrish and Lenski 1997, https://doi.org/10.1038/42701). The drift-barrier hypothesis says selection improves replication fidelity until limited by drift, explaining why mutation rates differ across species and why error-prone polymerases persist [A] (Lynch et al. 2016, https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg.2016.104). Lee et al. 2012 measured E. coli base-substitution and indel rates by whole-genome sequencing [M] (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1210309109). The fold increase of E. coli mutators (frequently reported as tens to hundreds of times) is unverified here.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: the cost of fidelity (more proofreading energy and time), the load of deleterious mutations. Heritable: the fidelity trait itself.
- **Trade-off**: Fidelity is costly; low fidelity builds up deleterious load. In stable environments selection pushes mu down until drift stops it; in changing, adapting populations mutators hitchhike up and later fall (Swings [A]).
- **Why it matters**: Without a heritable mutation rate, the kernel's mutation rate is a global dial; with one, evolvability becomes an evolved property, and lineages can differ in how fast they explore.
- **Sources**: Drake 1991 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.88.16.7160 ; Drake 1998 https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/148.4.1667 ; Sniegowski 1997 https://doi.org/10.1038/42701 ; Lynch 2016 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg.2016.104 ; Lee 2012 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1210309109 ; Swings 2017 https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.22939

## 16. Density and crowding: logistic growth, nutrient depletion, waste, cues

- **Primitive**: Density dependence can come from four separate physical mechanisms, and only some need a new law. (1) Nutrient depletion: already in Simolution (each cell's own resource, harvest depletes it). (2) Waste accumulation: a by-product field produced in proportion to metabolism (acetate, acid) that diffuses and decays and inhibits growth as a function of its concentration. (3) Direct cue: a signal field that cells emit and sense (quorum sensing) with no effect on the body except through what the genome does with it. (4) Contact: mechanical or contact inhibition (section 17). The logistic law is the shape of the emergent result of (1) and (2), not a primitive.
- **Numbers**: Monod's classical analysis of growth of bacterial cultures sets the nutrient-limited growth law [M] (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.mi.03.100149.002103). Acetate in E. coli batch culture: adding sodium acetate gave a logarithmic decrease in growth rate versus acetate concentration in the strains tested [A] (Luli and Strohl 1990, https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.56.4.1004-1011.1990). Stationary phase reviews: Navarro Llorens et al. 2010 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1574-6976.2010.00213.x); Finkel 2006 on survival and the GASP phenotype [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro1340). Quorum sensing in bacteria: Miller and Bassler 2001 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.micro.55.1.165). Contact inhibition of cell growth is classic in cell culture [M] (Eagle and Levine 1967, https://doi.org/10.1038/2131102a0). Inverse density dependence (Allee effect), where per-capita growth rises with density at low density [M] (Courchamp, Clutton-Brock and Grenfell 1999, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0169-5347(99)01683-3). Stewart and Franklin: chemical gradients in biofilms drive physiologically heterogeneous cells [A] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro1838). Density also protects cells from antibiotics (inoculum effect, section 8 [A]), and the density-dependent flip in the thermotaxis sign [A] (section 5) shows a signal that cells secrete can change behaviour.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: waste production and its toxicity function; diffusion and decay of signals. Heritable: waste tolerance, emission of signal, reading of signal, switching to a stationary state under starvation.
- **Trade-off**: Waste tolerance has a pump cost; signal emission has a production cost and exposes producers to cheaters (non-emitting cells that still use the signal). Crowding sensed through a diffusing signal is information about the neighbours, where waste is a shared tax on all.
- **Why it matters**: Waste fields give density dependence emergent from chemistry (no coded carrying capacity), while a cue field gives a communication channel with a real cost; both are standard, evolvable and low in code.
- **Sources**: Monod 1949 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.mi.03.100149.002103 ; Luli and Strohl 1990 https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.56.4.1004-1011.1990 ; Navarro Llorens 2010 https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1574-6976.2010.00213.x ; Finkel 2006 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro1340 ; Miller and Bassler 2001 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.micro.55.1.165 ; Eagle and Levine 1967 https://doi.org/10.1038/2131102a0 ; Courchamp 1999 https://doi.org/10.1016/S0169-5347(99)01683-3 ; Stewart and Franklin 2008 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro1838

## 17. Mechanical crowding: pushing, jamming, solid stress

- **Primitive**: Growing cells in a confined lattice must push neighbours to make room. Where Simolution forbids a birth with no free neighbour cell, real colonies instead build up mechanical pressure that slows growth, and cells at the colony edge or at the top (in 3D) get pushed out. A pressure variable per cell (number of occupied neighbours or accumulated displacement) can reduce growth rate or add a cost, and can push a unit to an adjacent cell.
- **Numbers**: Self-driven jamming: in growing microbial populations, dense growth builds up contact forces that the population generates itself and that confine it; the paper traces how single-cell pushing forces combine into collective confinement and slows growth in the interior [A] (Delarue et al. 2016, https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3741; I read the abstract only up to the mechanism claim, the growth-slowing quantification is in the full text, [M] for the details). In biofilms of up to 10,000 cells, the shape and order of 3D growing biofilms arise from an effective cell-cell interaction potential [A] (Hartmann et al. 2019, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-018-0356-9). Volfson et al. 2008 on biomechanical ordering of dense cell populations [M] (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0706805105); Dell'Arciprete et al. 2018 on a 2D colony as an active nematic [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06370-3); solid stress inhibits growth of multicellular tumour spheroids [M] (Helmlinger et al. 1997, https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt0897-778). Nadell, Drescher and Foster 2016 on spatial structure, cooperation and competition in biofilms [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro.2016.84). Unverified: pressure value that halts growth.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: pressure rises with local packing; pushing is a physical consequence of growth. Heritable: cell stiffness, adhesion, extracellular matrix investment, shape.
- **Trade-off**: Matrix or adhesion costs energy but holds position and shields interior cells; stiffness raises pushing ability but costs mass.
- **Why it matters**: Replaces a hard "no free cell, no birth" with a physical pressure gradient, so expansion pushes lineages outward, producing range expansion and spatial segregation of lineages with no extra behaviour.
- **Sources**: Delarue 2016 https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3741 ; Hartmann 2019 https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-018-0356-9 ; Volfson 2008 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0706805105 ; Dell'Arciprete 2018 https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06370-3 ; Helmlinger 1997 https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt0897-778 ; Nadell 2016 https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro.2016.84

## 18. Fluctuating environments: bet hedging, sensing, plasticity, acclimation

- **Primitive**: The environment alternates between states (temperature, toxin present or absent, resource pulse) with some switching statistics. The only law is the environment's dynamics. Strategies available to the genome: (a) track the environment by sensing and responding, (b) switch phenotype stochastically at a heritable rate (bet hedging), (c) take a fixed middle phenotype. Long-run fitness is the geometric mean of growth across the sequence of environments, which is why a low-variance strategy can beat a higher-mean one.
- **Numbers**: Kussell and Leibler showed that stochastic switching can be favoured over sensing when the environment changes infrequently, that the optimal switching rates mimic the statistics of environmental change, and derived a relation between long-term growth rate and the information available about the environment [A] (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1114383). Cohen 1966 gave the classic optimal-reproduction result for a random environment [M] (https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(66)90188-3); Kussell et al. 2005 applied it to persistence [M] (https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.104.035352). Bet hedging evolved de novo in 2 of 12 bacterial lines under an environment that continually favoured new states, through rapid stochastic phenotype switching enabled by a final mutation after earlier contingent ones [A] (Beaumont et al. 2009, https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08504). Persister switching [A] (Balaban 2004, section 10). Further: Veening et al. 2008 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.micro.62.081307.163002), Acar et al. 2008 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.110), Simons 2011 on the thin empirical evidence for bet hedging [M] (https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2011.0176). Predictive adaptation: microbial regulatory networks can respond to a cue that precedes the real stress, as in Mitchell et al. 2009 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08112) and Tagkopoulos et al. 2008 [M] (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1154456). Acclimation is not always beneficial: E. coli acclimated at 41.5 C was less fit at 41.5 C than 32 C-acclimated cells [A] (Leroi 1994, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.91.5.1917). Unverified: how often real seasonal regimes cross the infrequent-change threshold.
- **Fixed vs heritable**: Fixed: environmental dynamics, delay and cost of a physiological switch, cost of dormancy. Heritable: switching rate, sensor and response gain, acclimation speed, dormancy depth.
- **Trade-off**: Sensing costs (sensor gene, internal machinery, reaction lag) and fails when cues are uncorrelated with the future; stochastic switching wastes part of the population in the wrong state every generation but needs no information. Which wins depends on change frequency and cue reliability (Kussell and Leibler [A]).
- **Why it matters**: Environment statistics (period, predictability, cue quality) become the control knobs of strategy diversity: low frequency favours switching, high frequency favours a fixed generalist, reliable cues favour sensing. One law-level feature (a tunable fluctuating field) generates most of the major strategy types.
- **Sources**: Kussell and Leibler 2005 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1114383 ; Kussell 2005 https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.104.035352 ; Cohen 1966 https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(66)90188-3 ; Beaumont 2009 https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08504 ; Veening 2008 https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.micro.62.081307.163002 ; Acar 2008 https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.110 ; Simons 2011 https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2011.0176 ; Mitchell 2009 https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08112 ; Tagkopoulos 2008 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1154456 ; Leroi 1994 https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.91.5.1917

## Top five for a simulator

Ranked by strategy diversity per unit of physics (one new field or one multiplier, small code, many emergent strategies).

1. **Temperature field with an Arrhenius multiplier plus a steep high-T collapse (sections 1 to 3)**: one scalar field, one multiplier, a few cardinal parameters; yields thermal niches, specialist versus generalist, seasonal timing and hotter-is-better trade-offs, and the same field feeds thermotaxis for free.
2. **Fluctuating field statistics as the control knob (section 18)**: no new law at all, only a time-varying inflow of any field; sets whether switching, sensing or a fixed generalist wins.
3. **Waste by-product field that inhibits growth (section 16)**: replaces a coded carrying capacity with chemistry; also acidifies, so it couples to pH (section 6) when that is added.
4. **Diffusing toxin field with Hill dose response, a resistance trait with a convex cost, and local detoxification (sections 8, 9, 11)**: one field, one curve, one cost; yields resistance gradients, cheaters versus producers, collateral-sensitivity cycles when two toxins exist.
5. **Stress-coupled mutation rate with a heritable gain (sections 14, 15)**: reuses the existing mutation machinery, adds one multiplier; makes evolvability itself an evolved, stress-dependent property.

Next tier: oxygen as fuel and poison (section 12), pressure and jamming in place of the hard no-free-cell rule (section 17), UV flux with repair economics (section 13), osmotic and pH cardinal axes (sections 6 and 7), tolerance and dormancy through activity-proportional damage (section 10).
